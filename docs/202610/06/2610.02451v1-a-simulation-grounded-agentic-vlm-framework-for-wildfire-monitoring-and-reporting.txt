WORK FOR WILDFIRE MONITORING AND REPORTING

Bart van Bloemen Waanders2, Bo Zhu1

ABSTRACT
Effective wildfire monitoring requires relating visual evidence to physical fire
dynamics, yet real videos with synchronized physical annotations are scarce and
high-fidelity 3D simulation is costly. We present a simulation-grounded vision-
language model (VLM) framework that automatically converts 2D wildfire simula-
tions into labeled video episodes. A fixed Blender mapping produces low-detail
3D proxies aligned with simulator terrain, fuel layout, fire activity, and wind
cues; controllable video generation supplies richer appearance. The proxies are
intermediate representations rather than finely rendered final scenes. Generated
videos and simulator labels form reusable multimodal memory for a training-free
multi-agent VLM system that retrieves reference episodes, reconciles visual and
memory-based predictions, and produces structured wildfire reports. On held-out
generated episodes, video memory achieves 51.5% exact four-tag accuracy, com-
pared with 22.6% for direct VLM querying and 16–17% for text-only memory; the
complete system achieves 77.3% accuracy on six simulator-derived report fields.
Component ablations, cross-generator tests, and three real-UAV evaluations as-
sess retrieval, reporting, generator changes, and observable monitoring tasks. The
framework connects automatic simulation-to-proxy conversion with memory-based
VLM reasoning under scarce real-world physical annotations.

INTRODUCTION
Wildfire monitoring and reporting are fundamentally about connecting what is seen to what is
happening physically in the wildland. Fire-spread simulators (e.g., Mandel et al. (2011); Finney
(1998); Mallet et al. (2009); Doyle et al. (2024)) encode how fuel, slope, moisture, and wind shape
propagation, and satellite, aerial, and UAV measurements provide partial visual observations of active
incidents. Modern vision-language models (VLMs) need a framework to align two forms of evidence
in a shared representation. Real wildfire videos are visually rich, yet labels for wind, moisture, slope,
fuel condition, burn extent, and severe-fire behavior are usually absent. Simulators expose these
variables directly, yet their native outputs are grid states, masks, and fire fronts, which differ from the
aerial-view videos that VLMs are expected to interpret.
Though existing wildfire VLM work has advanced fire and smoke recognition, UAV monitoring,
satellite risk analysis, and VLM-guided planning Du et al. (2025); Wu et al. (2025); Seidel et al.
(2025); Ayanzadeh et al. (2026); Webb et al. (2026); Xie et al. (2025); Sandeep et al. (2026),
temporally rich videos paired with physical fire-state labels remain scarce. Collecting synchronized
wind, moisture, slope, fuel, and fire-behavior annotations is hazardous, expensive, and hard to control.
Available UAV datasets provide valuable detection and segmentation annotations Shamsoshoara
et al. (2021); Kularatne et al. (2024); Pesonen et al. (2025), but the annotations used here do not
jointly describe these drivers. Fire-spread simulators expose such conditions without providing
camera-view observations, while full 3D fire simulation Kokosza et al. (2024); Hädrich et al. (2021)
incurs costs that limit coverage across fuels, terrains, weather, and cameras. We therefore study a
scalable middle ground: efficient 2D simulations over real terrain and fuel maps are automatically
lifted into lightweight Blender proxies, translated into controlled videos, and stored as physics-labeled
memory for VLM reasoning.
Recent developments such as Code World Model (Chen et al., 2026b) and Blender scene authoring
with GPT-6 Astra highlight the potential of executable representations for visual world construction.

We explore this direction in a simulation-grounded VLM framework for wildfire monitoring and
reporting (Figure 1). Each episode starts with a 2D simulation over real LANDFIRE terrain and fuel
data spanning approximately 3 km. A fully automatic Blender procedure maps evolving simulator
fields into terrain, vegetation, flame, and wind-driven smoke cues. This lightweight proxy is an
intermediate representation: fixed conversion rules maintain simulator-to-scene correspondence,
while depth, scribble, and prompt controls guide richer video appearance. Generated clips retain their
prompts, simulator parameters, environmental tags, and fire-behavior labels as reusable multimodal
memory. Retrieved episodes then complement direct visual reasoning for interpreting new wildfire
videos without updating VLM weights. Section 6 further discusses the connections to code-based
world construction and general-purpose scene authoring.
Generated videos with known simulator labels provide controlled evaluation targets. We use 500 geo-
disjoint locations: 400 episodes populate memory and 100 are held out for evaluation (Appendix A).
For each query, retrieved references complement direct visual reasoning, and a judge resolves reporter
disagreements. Video memory achieves 51.5% exact four-tag accuracy versus 16–17% for text-only
memory and 22.6% for direct VLM querying; reporting reaches 77.3% on six simulator-derived
fields. Structured records retain predictions, evidence, and reasoning. Cross-generator and real-UAV
tests additionally assess generator changes, smoke/fire detection, and visible fire-area assessment.
Our contributions are:
• Automatic simulation-grounded visual evidence. We connect efficient 2D wildfire simulation
to controllable video generation through a fully automatic Blender proxy. A fixed mapping
expresses terrain, fuel layout, fire activity, and wind cues as lightweight 3D controls, constructing
labeled visual episodes under scarce real-world physical annotations.
• Memory-augmented agentic reporting. We organize generated videos and simulator context
as reusable multimodal memory, and combine retrieval and reranking with complementary
VLM reporters and selective disagreement adjudication. The training-free system produces
environmental-driver estimates and structured wildfire reports without updating model weights.
• Controlled evaluation with complementary real-UAV tests. Video memory achieves 51.5%
exact four-tag accuracy versus 22.6% for direct VLM querying and 16–17% for text-only memory;
the full reporter reaches 77.3% on six simulator-derived fields. Ablations, cross-generator tests,
and real-UAV perceptual evaluations examine the framework’s components and transfer beyond
the primary generated-video setting.

METHOD
Figure 1 gives the overall workflow. The framework has two main components: a simulation-
grounded visual evidence construction method and a memory-augmented multi-agent VLM system
for monitoring and reporting. The subsections below follow this order.
3.1
SIMULATION-GROUNDED VISUAL EVIDENCE CONSTRUCTION
The first stage constructs labeled visual episodes to reduce reliance on scarce real footage with
synchronized physical annotations. We retain the scalability and annotations of 2D wildfire simulation
while making its outputs visually accessible to VLMs: diverse episodes over real terrain and fuel maps
are lifted into procedural 3D proxies and translated into aerial-view clips. Here, simulation-grounded
refers to the simulator-derived labels and controls linking each episode to its originating scenario;
visual and temporal cue alignment is evaluated separately in Section 5.1.
3.1.1
PHYSICS-GROUNDED 2D WILDFIRE SIMULATION
To balance physical grounding and generation throughput, we use SimFire Doyle et al. (2024) as our
2D wildfire simulation platform. SimFire follows the classical Rothermel fire-spread model Rothermel
(1972) detailed in Appendix B.1, allowing us to generate large numbers of wildfire episodes with
meaningful macroscopic spread behavior. For each episode, we randomly sample a geographic

location specified by latitude and longitude, and query the LANDFIRE database to obtain real U.S.
fuel and terrain information. Fuel types are represented using the Anderson 13 fuel model Anderson
(1982), while terrain elevation is used to derive slope and support later 3D proxy construction. Wind
direction, wind-related parameters, and fuel-moisture settings are randomized to increase scenario
diversity. Each simulation covers a region of approximately 3 km in spatial extent. We refer readers
to Appendix B and C for implementation details.
Each episode is summarized using four physically meaningful factors: wind speed, moisture, slope
angle, and vegetation density. Each factor is discretized into low and high levels, yielding a compact
set of simulator-derived tags. These tags provide coarse environmental regimes for retrieval-based
state estimation and agent reasoning, rather than continuous measurements of a query incident.
Tag thresholds are specified in Appendix C; the six downstream report-field definitions appear in
Appendix A.
3.1.2
PROCEDURAL 3D PROXY
As shown in Figure 1, the 2D simulator produces fire masks, fuel maps, terrain fields, and fire-front
states, but these outputs are simple 2D projections onto the terrain topography and therefore do not
provide a 3D visual image similar to UAV video footage. We therefore design a Blender pipeline that
automatically construct a lightweight 3D proxy scene for each simulation. Elevation is converted into
a height-field terrain mesh; Anderson 13 fuel categories are mapped to procedural vegetation and
terrain textures; burning cells are augmented with flame billboards; and smoke billboards are oriented
and animated according to wind speed and direction. The proxy is not intended to be photorealistic.
Its role is to encode simulator-derived spatial structure—terrain shape, vegetation distribution, active
fire location, and wind-conditioned smoke direction—in a form that can guide video generation. For
details of fuel models mapping with billboard assets, we refer readers to Appendix B.2.
3.1.3
CONTROLLABLE VIDEO GENERATION
The proxy rendering encodes simulator structure but remains visually simple. We use controllable
video generation to translate it into a visually richer wildfire clip. Following VACE Jiang et al.
(2025), the generator is conditioned on a depth sequence Ranftl et al. (2020); Yang et al. (2024),
a scribble sequence Chan et al. (2022), and a text prompt. The depth and scribble controls guide
geometry, vegetation layout, fire-front location, and camera-consistent structure, while the prompt
specifies appearance-level information such as smoke density, flame visibility, vegetation type, and
wind-driven motion. The prompt is synthesized from simulation metadata and then refined by a
VLM (see Appendix F for details). We also use parameter-conditioned negative prompts to suppress
conflicting attributes, such as strong wind for low-wind cases or still smoke for high-wind cases.
Each generated video retains its originating simulator labels. Its observable cues reflect both the
proxy controls and metadata-conditioned prompts; cue agreement is assessed rather than assumed.
3.2
MEMORY-AUGMENTED AGENTIC VLM MONITORING AND REPORTING
The second stage uses the generated video episodes as physics-labeled visual memory for wildfire
state estimation and reporting. The design goal is to keep the system training-free: new simulator
episodes update the memory bank, not the VLM weights. As shown in Figure 3, given a query

3.2.1
PHYSICS-LABELED MULTIMODAL MEMORY
Each memory entry is a key–value item. The key is a multimodal embedding of the generated
wildfire video and its simulation-derived text description. The value stores the generated video, the
prompt used to produce it, the 2D simulation parameters, the environmental tags, and downstream
fire-behavior labels. This structure makes the memory both searchable and interpretable: retrieval
returns not only similar videos, but also the simulator conditions associated with those reference
episodes, not ground-truth measurements of the query.
At query time, the same multimodal embedder maps the input video into the shared embedding space.
We compute cosine similarity between the query embedding and each memory key and retrieve the
top-k candidate episodes. The retrieved examples are then used as in-context evidence for estimating
the query’s wind, moisture, slope, and vegetation tags. In practice (see Section 5.2), neither the agent
alone nor the embedder alone yields sufficiently accurate classification across these attributes. We
therefore complement embedding-based retrieval with candidate reranking before using retrieved
labels for query estimation.
3.2.2
MEMORY-AUGMENTED MULTI-AGENT REPORTING
We formulate downstream wildfire understanding as an agentic VLM reporting problem combining
query observations with retrieved physics-labeled references. Given a query video, the system
produces a structured report over fire-behavior fields such as spread risk, burn extent, vegetation
condition, and severe-fire indicators. Rather than relying on a single model, we separate report
generation into four stages: memory retrieval, multi-agent reporting, disagreement adjudication, and
report rendering Lewis et al. (2020); Wu et al. (2024a); Chan et al. (2023).
For a query video q, we retrieve the top-k most relevant episodes from the memory split. Retrieved
episodes supply environmental driver tags, physical summaries, and downstream labels. For each
binary report field t, the retrieved examples define a score-weighted memory consensus for adjudi-

VALIDATION AND RESULTS
We assess proxy–video alignment, environmental-driver prediction, and six-field reporting on gener-
ated episodes with known simulator targets, then test generator changes and real-UAV smoke/fire
detection and visible fire-area assessment.
5.1
SIMULATION-TO-OBSERVATION RESULTS
Figures 2 and 5 show simulation-to-video construction and tag-conditioned appearances; additional
examples appear in Figure 10 and the supplement. Against mismatched proxies, correct-pair SSIM
and LPIPS win rates are 76.6% and 99.1%, with mean smoke-plume-axis and growth-front-axis
errors of 7.6◦and 35.8◦(Appendix D). GPT-5.6-luna rates agreement across fire-front location, fuel
distribution, burned area, and terrain at 75.00/100 (Appendix D.1). These results support partial
visual-cue alignment, not preservation of physical spread rates or burned-area trajectories.
5.2
MEMORY-BASED ENVIRONMENTAL DRIVER PREDICTION
Table 3 compares exact four-tag accuracy and the proportions with at least three or two correct tags on
held-out generated videos. Reranked memory reaches 51.5%, versus 22.6% for the strongest query-
only VLM. With 16 labeled reference videos covering all tag combinations, the strongest example-
augmented baseline achieves 27.27%; query simulator metadata remains withheld (Appendix G.5).
5.3
MEMORY GROWTH EFFECTS
Increasing the memory bank from 100 to 400 episodes improves exact four-tag accuracy from 26.3%
to 51.5% (Figure 7). This supports the training-free memory design: adding simulator-grounded
episodes broadens the available reference coverage without updating VLM weights.

DISCUSSION
Relationship to Code World Model.
Code World Model (Chen et al., 2026b) and our framework
combine explicit state evolution, spatial proxies, and generative visualization. Code World Model
uses a coding agent to maintain persistent state and revise mechanisms for interaction. We instead
map prescribed wildfire trajectories to aligned aerial-view episodes through a fixed simulator-to-proxy
converter, using these labeled episodes as multimodal memory for interpreting query videos.
Fixed conversion versus scene authoring.
GPT-6 Astra can author Blender scenes and could
help develop our converter. New episodes, however, change inputs rather than code. The proxy
prioritizes simulator alignment and low rendering overhead, leaving appearance detail to video
generation. Reuse avoids per-episode scene-authoring calls and token costs; other inference costs
remain. Like Code World Model, we separate high-level reasoning from repeated execution: the
distinction concerns where reasoning is needed, not a measured speed or cost advantage.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Limitations.
Real-incident environmental-driver estimation still requires validation against syn-
chronized measurements: our real-UAV tests assess smoke/fire detection and visible fire area, whereas
physical-label targets come from simulation. The fixed Blender mapping is domain-specific; new
simulator fields or scene types may require extending its rules and assets. Spatial correspondence
does not make billboard smoke a 3D physical solution or ensure that generated videos preserve proxy
dynamics. Metadata-conditioned prompts may introduce tag-specific cues, and footage may omit
sensor noise, camera motion, long-horizon evolution, or rare extremes. The coarse simulator and
tags omit coupled fire–atmosphere dynamics, spotting, suppression, and changing weather. Retrieval
depends on memory coverage, reporter agreement does not guarantee correctness, and reports require
expert validation before operational use.
Future Work.
Future work should calibrate generated observations against physically annotated
real footage, expand scenario coverage, and involve wildfire experts. Further tests should isolate
prompt conditioning, compare reporting with matched evidence and compute, and measure stage-wise
costs against agent-authored proxy alternatives. Integration with hazard-adaptation and UAV planning
frameworks Xie et al. (2025); Hyun et al. (2025); Webb et al. (2026) requires task-specific validation.
8
CONCLUSION
We presented a simulation-grounded agentic VLM framework for wildfire monitoring and reporting.
A fully automatic Blender conversion expresses 2D wildfire states as lightweight, simulation-aligned
proxies; controlled video generation produces labeled episodes for multimodal memory. Retrieved
references and complementary reporters support environmental-driver estimation and structured
reporting without updating VLM weights. Controlled, cross-generator, and real-UAV perceptual tests
support this use of simulation experience under scarce physical annotations. Real-world physical
calibration and expert validation remain important next steps.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
In this section, we provide additional implementation details of the proposed framework.
2D Simulation.
For the 2D simulation stage, we use SimFire Doyle et al. (2024). All simulations
are conducted on a 3 km domain, with each grid cell corresponding to approximately 30 m. The
simulation horizon is set to represent one hour of wildfire propagation. For the four major factors
affecting the simulations, we define low wind speed as below 3 mph and high wind speed as above
3 mph. Low moisture is defined in the range [0.0001, 0.005), while high moisture is defined in the
range [0.005, 0.01]. For vegetation coverage and slope, we first query real-world fuel maps and terrain
data for each location, and then perform post hoc categorization based on the resulting measurements.
Under this procedure, the dataset is approximately divided into low vegetation coverage for values
below 6.96%, and high vegetation coverage for values between 6.96% and 70.77%. Similarly, low
slope corresponds to values between 0◦and 9.10◦, while high slope corresponds to values between
9.10◦and 34.94◦. We additionally show the queried 500 physical locations in Figure 9.
Figure 9: Geographic locations for memory construction and held-out evaluation; no VLM weights
are updated.
19


Preprint. Under review.
3D Rendering.
We use Blender to perform proxy 2D-to-3D rendering. For geometry, the elevation
fields queried from LANDFIRE are converted into 3D terrain surfaces. For vegetation, following the
Anderson 13 fuel model, we collect multiple vegetation and terrain textures and represent different
fuel types using billboards. If a grid cell is labeled as burning in the 2D simulation, we randomly
sample fire billboards within that cell and additionally render tilted smoke billboards according to
the wind speed and wind direction. For camera placement, we choose aerial viewpoints that can
observe the initial ignition region while resembling footage captured by a UAV. Finally, all videos are
rendered in Blender using the Eevee renderer.
3D Generation.
We use VACE Jiang et al. (2025) as the video generation backbone. Following the
preprocessing strategy of Chen et al. (2025), we compute both depth and scribble control videos from
proxy videos rendered in Blender. To better align the controls with the domain expected by VACE,
we estimate depth using Yang et al. (2024) and extract scribble signals using Chan et al. (2022). To
screen depth alignment, we measure the Spearman correlation between the generated video depth
and the proxy-rendered depth. If the score is below 0.35, we retry generation up to three times; if all
retries fail, we resample a new location and repeat the full generation pipeline.
Memory System.
We use Qwen3-2B-VL as the embedding model. As shown in Sec. 4, this model
provides more accurate results than the other embedding models considered in our setting. We use
GPT-5 as the reranker; the corresponding prompt is provided in Appendix G.4.
Multi-Agent Reporting
The memory split supplies reference episodes and train-derived lookup
priors without updating VLM weights. The top k = 5 retrieved episodes are aggregated to compute
task-wise consensus scores and metadata summaries for adjudication, whereas four-tag prediction
in Table 3 uses the top k = 1 retrieved episode. The three reporters correspond to different prompt
contexts: the visual reporter receives only sampled query frames; the lookup reporter receives query
frames and the top retrieved tag-bundle prior; and the combined-memory reporter receives query
frames, the lookup prior, and retrieved simulator-derived summaries. For each report field, unanimous
reporter outputs are copied directly into the final prediction vector, while disputed fields are sent to the
judge. The judge prompt includes the sampled frames, reporter predictions, short rationales, locked
unanimous fields, top-k memory consensus, and retrieved metadata summaries, and is constrained
to revise only disputed fields. The resolved predictions, routing decisions, rationales, and retrieval
summaries are serialized and rendered into an HTML report for each query.

You are the direct visual specialist in a wildfire multi-agent

Visual Report Agent Task Rubrics
Predict all downstream task keys from visual evidence only:
- spread_fast_bin: Fast Spread Risk: choose `fast_or_very_fast` only

substantially over the sampled frames. Choose `slow_or_moderate` for
compact, slowly changing, or ambiguous fronts.
- peak_high_bin: High Peak Burning Area: choose `high` only when
active burning visibly covers a broad continuous landscape-scale area
likely >= 90,000 m^2. Choose `low` when the active flaming footprint
is localized or uncertain.
- burnscar_high_bin: High Final Burnscar: choose `high` only when

>= 300,000 m^2. Do not infer high burnscar from smoke alone.

only when both visible fast spread and a large final burned footprint
are supported. If either condition is uncertain, choose `low`.
- severe_fire_behavior: Severe Fire Behavior: choose `high` only if at
least two visual indicators are supported: fast spread, large active
burning area, large final burnscar. Choose `low` if only one or none

L.1
CROSS-GENERATOR EVALUATION
We use LTX-2.3 + AVControl as a video-generation backbone independent of the original VACE
generator. We evaluate both LTX-generated queries against VACE-generated memory and VACE-
generated queries against LTX-generated memory. Results are reported in Table 5a. Our method
reaches 41.00% and 44.44% accuracy, respectively, compared with 28.00% and 27.27% for the
strongest direct VLM baselines in the two query sets. This tests changing the video-generation
backbone, not changing the physical simulator or procedural proxy renderer. It isolates a visual-
generator change from the separate real-UAV tests below.
L.2
EXTERNAL UAV EVALUATION
Real wildfire videos jointly annotated with environmental drivers and fire behavior are scarce,
motivating the simulation-based construction of labeled reference episodes. We complement the
controlled physical-label evaluation with real-UAV tests using available annotations: smoke detection,
fire presence, and visible fire-area assessment. These tests assess observable monitoring tasks;
the annotations used here do not jointly specify the four environmental drivers needed to validate
physical-driver recovery.

Fire Pesonen et al. (2025). Table 23 reports accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. Our method, Claude
4, and ForestFireVLM-7B reach 100% on all four metrics; this evaluation does not distinguish among
those methods.
