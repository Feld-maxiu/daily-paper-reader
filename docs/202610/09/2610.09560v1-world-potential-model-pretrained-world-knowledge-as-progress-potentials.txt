## Abstract 

Long-horizon language agents often receive supervision only from terminal task outcomes, leaving little signal for distinguishing productive intermediate behavior from stagnation or even regression. Rather than learning a separate value function or process reward model for every task, we ask whether pretrained models can recognize task progress from their existing world knowledge. We formalize this capability with a World Potential Model (WPM ), a goal-conditioned evaluator of task-relative realized progress in agent contexts. In ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, off-the-shelf pretrained models substantially outperform chance at recovering realized-progress structure without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. We further anchor these progress judgments to task-specific milestones to obtain scalar world potentials , whose temporal differences provide process-sensitive step-level credit for policy optimization. Under matched comparisons, WPM -guided optimization improves success over outcome-only GRPO across all evaluated configurations. Together, these results provide initial evidence that pretrained world knowledge can support reusable realized-progress evaluation and provide useful supervision for long-horizon agents.

1arXiv:2610.09560v1 [cs.AI] 7 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly becoming the cognitive core of agentic systems [1], where they interpret environments [2], invoke tools [3, 4], and drive multi-step reasoning [5–7] across increasingly complex tasks. As these tasks become longer-horizon, however, terminal outcomes alone provide too little structure for evaluating intermediate processes [8–15]. A failed trajectory may have made substantial progress before a late mistake, while a successful trajectory may contain redundant exploration, prolonged stagnation, or temporary regression. Long-horizon agents therefore need a way to distinguish productive intermediate behavior before the final outcome is known. Value functions and process reward models provide one route to denser supervision by estimating future return or scoring intermediate behavior [16, 17]. These evaluators can be effective, but typically rely on task-specific annotation and training. For agents expected to operate across heterogeneous tasks and environments, repeatedly constructing a new evaluator may be expensive and difficult to scale. This raises a complementary question: must progress evaluation itself require learning a task-specific evaluator, or can it be elicited from knowledge already acquired during pretraining? 

Pretrained foundation models encounter broad descriptions of procedures, physical interactions, causal relationships, and the structure of complex tasks [18–21]. Such knowledge provides a plausible basis for recognizing not only whether an individual action is plausible, but also what has already been accomplished in an evolving agent context. For example, acquiring a required object, resolving a constraint, or completing a necessary subprocedure may represent meaningful progress, whereas undoing a satisfied condition may represent regression. This suggests that pretrained models may support a reusable capability for recognizing realized task progress without task-specific evaluator training. We formalize this hypothesized capability through the abstraction of a World Potential Model (WPM ). Given a task goal, a WPM evaluates the goal-relevant progress already realized in an agent context using pretrained world knowledge. We call the resulting task-relative progress structure world potential . Because globally calibrated scalar progress is difficult to define across open-ended tasks, we take ordinal judgments of relative progress as the primary interface to this structure. To obtain a scalar potential for downstream use, we introduce one concrete instantiation that anchors these judgments to ordered task-specific milestones. The resulting task-local potentials can then provide step-level progress signals through their temporal differences. We study the WPM abstraction and its downstream utility in ALFWorld [22] and ScienceWorld [23], two structured long-horizon environments in which privileged progress signals enable controlled evaluation. Across both environments, off-the-shelf pretrained models substantially outperform chance at recovering realized-progress structure without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning, providing evidence for the existence of the progress-recognition capability underlying WPM s. We further find that, when these judgments are anchored to task-specific milestones, the resulting scalar potentials provide useful downstream supervision: under matched comparisons, WPM -guided optimization improves success over outcome-only GRPO across all evaluated configurations. This paper makes three contributions. First, we introduce the World Potential Model , a general abstraction for eliciting task-relative realized progress from pretrained world knowledge. Second, we develop a practical framework for turning realized-progress judgments into task-local scalar potentials for long-horizon agents. Third, we show how world potentials can provide process-sensitive supervision for sparse-reward policy optimization. Together, our results provide initial evidence that realized-progress evaluation is a reusable capability of pretrained models and a useful source of supervision for long-horizon agents.

2.2 World Potential Models 

World potential. For a task goal 𝑔 , a World Potential Model (WPM ) is an evaluator that uses pretrained world knowledge to assess an agent context 𝑥 in terms of the goal-relevant progress already realized toward 

𝑔 . We refer to the resulting task-relative progress structure as world potential . The target is retrospective :it characterizes what has already been accomplished in the current context, rather than predicting future 

return . A WPM need not directly produce a globally calibrated numerical progress score. Instead, we take ordinal judgments of relative realized progress as its primary interface, and introduce a task-local scalar representation only when needed for downstream use. 

Ordinal progress interface. Given two contexts 𝑥 𝑖 and 𝑥 𝑗 from the same task instance, a WPM compares their relative realized progress. We write 

𝑥 𝑖 ⪰𝑔 𝑥 𝑗 , (4) when the WPM judges 𝑥 𝑖 to embody at least as much goal-relevant realized progress toward 𝑔 as 𝑥 𝑗 . Relative progress can be meaningful even when an absolute progress score is not. For example, after an agent acquires a necessary clue, it may be natural to judge the resulting context as representing greater realized progress than the preceding one, while assigning either context a globally meaningful score such as “0.63 complete” is considerably less well-defined. The relation is task-relative: a WPM need not place unrelated tasks on a common numerical scale, but only compare realized progress within the task being evaluated. 2.4 Relation to Existing Abstractions 

World Potential Model s are related to value functions , reward models , and world models , but differ in what they evaluate and how that evaluation is obtained. 

Value functions. Both value functions and WPM s evaluate an agent context or state, but their targets differ fundamentally. A policy-conditioned value function 𝑉 𝜋 (𝑥 ) is prospective : it estimates the expected future return 

obtainable from 𝑥 under policy 𝜋 , and therefore generally depends on the policy being evaluated. A WPM 

is retrospective : it evaluates the goal-relevant progress already realized in the current context rather than predicting future return . This progress judgment is defined independently of the policy that produced the context. Two contexts may therefore embody similar realized progress even if their future prospects under a particular policy differ substantially. 

Reward and process-reward models. World potentials can be converted into reward-like signals, for example through temporal differences such as 𝛾 Φ𝑔 (𝑥 𝑡 +1) − Φ𝑔 (𝑥 𝑡 ), but the WPM itself is an evaluator of realized progress rather than a reward model trained for a particular task. Conventional reward or process-reward models 

are typically fitted from task-specific labels, preferences, or rollouts. In our setting, the evaluator parameters receive no task-specific fine-tuning; instead, progress judgments are elicited from a pretrained model and, for the scalar instantiation above, referenced to task-specific milestone anchors. Thus, task-specific structure enters through the scalarization reference frame rather than through evaluator parameter training. 

World models. Despite the shared term world , a WPM is not a model of environment dynamics. Conventional 

world models predict transitions, future observations, or other consequences of actions, for example through 

𝑝 (𝑠 𝑡 +1 | 𝑠 𝑡 , 𝑎 𝑡 ). A WPM neither predicts transitions nor simulates future states. It evaluates the realized progress represented in the current context. ## 3 Do Pretrained Models Expose World-Potential Structure? 

The central empirical question behind the WPM abstraction is whether pretrained models can recover meaningful task-relative progress structure from agent contexts without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. We evaluate this question at the two levels introduced in Section 2. Pairwise progress comparison directly tests whether pretrained models can make the relative progress judgments that constitute the WPM ’s primary ordinal interface. Anchored progress localization further tests whether these judgments can be referenced to a fixed task-specific milestone ladder to obtain the task-local localization used in the scalar construction. 

3.1 Evaluation Tasks 

Pairwise progress comparison. Given two agent contexts 𝑥 𝑖 and 𝑥 𝑗 associated with the same task goal 𝑔 ,the model predicts which context embodies greater goal-relevant progress already realized toward task completion. The two contexts may arise from different interaction trajectories and need not share a common prefix. The task therefore requires comparing the realized progress represented in the contexts rather than relying on their temporal positions within a trajectory. No milestone ladder is provided, so this evaluation directly probes the ordinal progress interface defined in Section 2.2. 

Anchored progress localization. Given an agent context 𝑥 𝑡 together with its fixed task-specific milestone ladder 𝑀 𝑔 , the model predicts the anchor index 𝑧 𝑡 corresponding to the context’s realized-progress level. The number of anchors is determined during task-specific milestone construction and may vary across tasks. The corresponding scalar world potential is then given by the fixed anchor coordinate 𝜙 𝑧 𝑡 . This evaluation therefore tests the anchored scalar representation introduced in Section 2.3 rather than requiring the model to assign an unconstrained numerical progress score directly to the context. Both tasks are formulated as categorical prediction problems. The exact evaluation prompts are provided in Appendix A.1. 

3.2 Experimental Setting 

Benchmarks and labels. We instantiate both evaluation tasks in ALFWorld [22] and ScienceWorld [23], two long-horizon interactive environments in which successful task completion requires multiple stages of meaningful intermediate progress. Both environments provide privileged signals for constructing environment-grounded progress labels. In ALFWorld, we use normalized remaining expert-plan length as a planner-based proxy : although formally a cost-to-go measure, manual inspection indicates that its ordering tracks relative realized progress, and we filter near-tied states. In ScienceWorld, the native task score directly awards partial credit for satisfied goals and subgoals. These signals are used only for evaluation-label construction and are never provided to the models; Appendix A.3 gives full details. 5Models and evaluation protocol. We evaluate seven language and vision-language models from three model families: MiMo, Qwen2.5, and Qwen3. All models are tested under the same prompting protocol and without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. We measure accuracy against environment-grounded labels. For an evaluation instance with 𝐶 𝑖 candidate labels, uniform random guessing has expected accuracy 1 /𝐶 𝑖 .Pairwise comparison therefore has a fixed chance accuracy of 50%, whereas anchored progress localization has a task-dependent chance accuracy because the number of milestone anchors varies across tasks. The mean random-guess accuracies are 20 .00% in ALFWorld and 15 .27% in ScienceWorld. 

3.3 Results 

Table 1 reports performance on pairwise progress comparison and anchored progress localization .

Relative realized progress is strongly recoverable in these settings. All evaluated models substantially outperform the 50% random baseline on pairwise progress comparison. Averaged across models and environments, pairwise accuracy reaches 92 .52%, with the best results reaching 95 .33% on ALFWorld and 98 .00% on ScienceWorld. These results provide direct evidence that pretrained models can often determine which of two partially completed agent contexts embodies greater goal-relevant realized progress, even when the contexts arise from different interaction paths. The result is consistent across all three evaluated model families, both language-only and vision-language models, and both environments, suggesting that it is not specific to a single model or benchmark. 

Anchored progress localization is harder but remains informative. Average localization accuracy across models and environments reaches 74 .71%, with all evaluated models exceeding the corresponding random baselines of 20 .00% in ALFWorld and 15 .27% in ScienceWorld. The best results reach 88 .00% and 85 .33%, respectively. Although localization accuracy is lower than pairwise accuracy, this gap is consistent with the different structure of the two evaluations: pairwise comparison requires resolving a single relative-progress relation, whereas anchored localization requires placing a context among multiple ordered, task-dependent progress anchors. These results show that pretrained models can meaningfully localize agent contexts relative to fixed task-specific milestone ladders, providing the localization step required by the scalar construction in Section 2.3. 

Takeaway. The pairwise results support the core premise of the WPM abstraction: in two controlled long-horizon environments, off-the-shelf pretrained models recover substantial task-relative ordinal realized-progress structure without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. Anchored localization is less accurate but remains substantially above chance, showing that these judgments can also be referenced to task-specific progress anchors for the scalar construction introduced in Section 2.3. We next ask whether world potentials constructed from these imperfect judgments provide useful downstream supervision.

## 5 A Utility Case Study in Sparse-Reward Policy Optimization 

Section 3 shows that pretrained models can recover meaningful realized-progress structure from agent contexts, while Section 4 converts anchored world potentials into process-sensitive step-level credit. We now ask whether this credit provides useful supervision for policy learning under sparse terminal rewards. We compare WPM -guided optimization with outcome-only GRPO under matched actor-training configurations, using task success as the downstream evaluation criterion. This experiment is intended as a controlled test of the utility of WPM -derived process-sensitive credit rather than as a comprehensive comparison of reinforcement learning algorithms. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Environments and data splits. We evaluate on the same two long-horizon interactive environments used in Section 3: ALFWorld and ScienceWorld. For ALFWorld, policies are trained on the official training split and evaluated on valid_unseen . For ScienceWorld, policies are trained on the official training variations and evaluated on the corresponding test variations. Both environments provide sparse terminal supervision. 

Policies and controlled comparison. We optimize Qwen2.5-Instruct models at 3B and 7B scale and Qwen3 at 4B scale. For reference, we also report the corresponding vanilla checkpoints. Our primary comparison is between outcome-only GRPO and WPM -guided optimization. For each actor model, the two optimization methods use matched task sampling, rollout construction, and actor-update configurations; they differ in the learning signal and resulting credit construction. Outcome-only GRPO derives trajectory-level advantages from terminal task outcomes, whereas WPM -guided optimization uses the process-sensitive step-level returns defined in Section 4. World potentials are produced by a frozen Qwen3-VL-32B evaluator using the fixed task-specific milestone ladders described in Section 2.3. The evaluator parameters remain fixed throughout actor training. For each actor–environment configuration, we run both methods with the same three random seeds and report mean performance across the three runs. 

Evaluation metric. We report task success rate, defined as the percentage of evaluation episodes whose final environment state satisfies the task goal. ## 6 Related Work 

Process supervision and progress-based credit. Process supervision provides intermediate learning signals beyond terminal correctness [17]. Recent work extends this idea to interactive agents through learned process reward models, rollout-derived credit, and subgoal- or progress-based supervision [15, 31–35]. These approaches reinforce the importance of intermediate credit in long-horizon tasks, but obtain such credit from different sources, including policy rollouts, learned evaluators, intrinsic beliefs, or explicit subgoal structure. Our focus is complementary: we ask whether a frozen pretrained model already exposes a task-relative notion of realized progress that can be elicited without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. A WPM therefore treats realized-progress evaluation as the object of study itself, with ordinal comparison as its primary interface, rather than defining progress through predicted future return. 

Foundation models for reward and progress construction. Pretrained language and vision-language models have been used to generate reward functions, provide reward judgments, and construct task-progress signals [20, 36–41]. These methods share with WPM s the premise that pretrained models contain task knowledge that can support downstream learning. Our formulation isolates a different interface to that knowledge: the pretrained evaluator makes ordinal judgments about progress already realized in an agent context, which can then be referenced to task-specific anchors when a scalar potential is needed. This separates the general progress-evaluation capability from the particular scalarization and optimization scheme used downstream. Our policy-optimization construction is also related to potential-based reward shaping [42]; however, for 0 < 𝜌 < 1, intermediate world potentials remain explicitly represented in the process-sensitive return, so the resulting objective is not the classical policy-invariant shaping construction. 97 Conclusion 

We introduced the World Potential Model (WPM ), an abstraction for eliciting task-relative realized progress from pretrained world knowledge. In ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, off-the-shelf pretrained models recover substantial realized-progress structure without task-specific evaluator fine-tuning. We further showed that these judgments can be anchored into scalar world potentials and converted into process-sensitive step-level credit. Under matched actor-training configurations, WPM -guided optimization improves task success over outcome-only GRPO across all evaluated settings. Together, these results provide initial evidence that realized-progress evaluation can serve as a reusable interface between pretrained world knowledge and long-horizon agent learning. Evaluating world potentials 

across broader tasks and agent settings remains an important direction.

A.1 WPM Progress-Judgment Interfaces 

As described in Section 2, we access world potential primarily through ordinal judgments rather than asking the model to directly produce a globally calibrated scalar score. Our implementation uses two prompt interfaces: pairwise progress comparison between agent contexts and anchored progress localization of a single context.

A.2 Task-Specific Anchors Construction 

The scalar construction in Section 2.3 requires a task-local set of ordered progress anchors 𝑀 𝑔 . The WPM formulation does not require a particular source for these anchors; here we describe the construction used in our experiments. For each task instance, we provide a pretrained model with the task description and one successful expert trajectory. The purpose of the trajectory is to expose a valid realization of the task from which the model can identify salient intermediate accomplishments. It is used only to construct the reference frame: it does not train or fine-tune the WPM evaluator, and it is not provided when the WPM subsequently evaluates agent-generated contexts. Importantly, the generated anchors describe task states rather than steps of the demonstration. We instruct the model to identify verifiable accomplishments that characterize increasing levels of goal-relevant realized progress, rather than reproducing the demonstrated action sequence. Consequently, the resulting ladder provides a sparse set of reference points rather than progress labels for the arbitrary contexts that the policy may later visit. Localizing such contexts relative to these anchors remains the responsibility of the frozen 

WPM .Concretely, the model generates between 4 and 10 intermediate anchors according to task complexity. Each anchor must (i) describe a salient accomplishment relevant to task completion, (ii) be state-based rather than tied to a particular action or trajectory position, and (iii) be verifiable from the agent-visible context. The model also assigns each anchor a scalar coordinate satisfying 0 = 𝜙 0 < 𝜙 1 < · · · < 𝜙 𝐾 = 1,

where 𝑚 0 denotes no meaningful task progress and 𝑚 𝐾 denotes task completion. Coordinates are intended to reflect the relative amount of goal-relevant progress represented by each accomplishment rather than being determined solely by its ordinal index. The resulting anchor set is generated once and fixed throughout capability evaluation and policy optimization. Thus, this instantiation uses a single successful demonstration to establish task-local calibration, while relying on pretrained knowledge to interpret previously unseen and potentially off-demonstration agent contexts relative to that calibration. A.3 Environment-Grounded Capability Evaluation Labels

Environment-grounded progress scores. For ALFWorld, we use the environment’s symbolic planner to measure remaining distance to task completion. Let 𝐿 𝐷 (𝑥 ) denote the length of an expert plan from the restored environment state associated with context 𝑥 to a goal-satisfying state. We define 

𝑃 ALF (𝑥 ) = 1 − 𝐿 𝐷 (𝑥 )

𝐿 𝐷 (𝑥 0) , (18) where 𝑥 0 is the initial context of the task instance. 𝑃 ALF is formally a normalized cost-to-go-based proxy, rather than a definition of retrospective realized progress itself. We additionally exclude near-tied states when constructing pairwise labels, as described below, to avoid treating small and potentially ambiguous differences in planner distance as reliable progress distinctions. As a qualitative sanity check, we manually inspected 100 randomly sampled context pairs and found that the planner-induced ordering was consistent with relative realized-progress judgments in 100% of the inspected pairs. Pairwise progress labels. For two contexts 𝑥 𝑖 and 𝑥 𝑗 from the same task instance, we label the context with the larger privileged progress score as having greater realized progress. Pairs with equal scores are discarded. For ALFWorld, we additionally retain a pair only when

𝑠 𝑘 = 𝑃 ( ¯ 𝑥 𝑘 ), (21) and use 𝑠 𝑘 as the environment-grounded progress level associated with anchor 𝑘 . For a sampled context 𝑥 ,the localization target is

## C Policy Optimization Implementation Details 

This appendix provides implementation details for the policy-optimization experiments in Section 5. The experiments compare outcome-only GRPO with WPM -guided optimization under a shared actor-training pipeline. The two methods use matched task sampling, rollout construction, policy models, and actor-update configurations, while differing in the learning signal and resulting credit construction. 

C.1 Environments and Task Sampling 

Benchmark documentation. We study two English-language interactive agent benchmarks with complemen-tary task domains. ALFWorld consists of compositional household navigation and object-manipulation tasks, including object placement, examination, cleaning, heating, and cooling. ScienceWorld is a text-based environment grounded in an elementary-school science curriculum and contains 30 task types spanning scientific procedures and reasoning involving topics such as changes of state, measurement, electrical conductivity, biology, chemistry, and forces. Both benchmarks require multi-step interaction with simulated environments rather than static language prediction. They contain no demographic groups or human-subject data relevant to our experiments. 

Data partitions and statistics. We use the official data partitions provided by ALFWorld and ScienceWorld and do not construct additional random train–evaluation splits. ALFWorld contains 3 , 553 training games, 140 valid_seen games, and 134 valid_unseen games. We sample policy-training tasks from the 3 , 553-game training split and evaluate on all 134 valid_unseen games, which contain held-out environments and task instances. ScienceWorld contains 7 , 207 task variations across 30 task types. Its official partitions contain 3, 589 training, 1 , 799 development, and 1 , 819 test variations. We sample policy-training tasks from the official training variations and evaluate on the official test variations; the development variations are not used in our policy-optimization experiments. The ScienceWorld splits are exposed by the environment through 

get_variations_train() , get_variations_dev() , and get_variations_test() .The dataset records supplied to our trainer serve only as rollout slots and should not be interpreted as additional benchmark examples or a new data split. Interactive task instances are sampled from the corresponding official benchmark partition when the environment is initialized or reset. 

C.2 Training Configuration and Software 

Training Configuration. We optimize Qwen2.5-Instruct-3B, Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B, and Qwen3-4B policies. Outcome-only GRPO and WPM -guided optimization use the same actor-training configuration. Each update samples 16 task groups with 8 policy rollouts per task, yielding 128 trajectories. Each rollout is capped at 30 environment actions, and the policy context retains the two most recent interaction steps. We use PPO-style clipped actor updates with KL regularization and the same invalid-action treatment for both methods. The learning rate is 1 × 10 −6, and training runs for 100 epochs. 

Software and implementations. Our released implementation targets Linux with NVIDIA GPUs and Python 3.12. The reproduction environment uses vLLM 0.11.0, FlashAttention 2.7.4.post1, Gymnasium 0.29.1, and Stable-Baselines3 2.6.0. We use the ALFWorld environment through its official Python package and the ScienceWorld implementation included in our released codebase. The policy checkpoints are specified through the exact Hugging Face model identifiers. World-potential evaluation and milestone generation use 23 OpenAI-compatible vLLM endpoints serving Qwen/Qwen3-VL-32B-Thinking-FP8. The released implemen-tation and experiment entry points are provided in our public repository. Our computational experiments were conducted using 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
